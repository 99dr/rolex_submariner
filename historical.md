# 2. Historical Background

## 2.1 Introduction of the Submariner in 1953

The Rolex Submariner was introduced in 1953 as a mechanical
wristwatch specifically developed for underwater activities. Its
creation marked the introduction of Rolex's first diving wristwatch
and represented an important development in the company's Oyster
Perpetual Professional collection.

The early 1950s were characterized by growing interest in underwater
exploration, recreational diving, and the development of specialized
equipment for marine activities. In this context, watch manufacturers
began exploring ways to adapt mechanical wristwatches to environments
involving increased water pressure, limited visibility, and demanding
operating conditions.

Rolex addressed these requirements by developing a watch that
combined an Oyster waterproof case, an automatic mechanical movement,
a highly legible dial, and a rotating bezel designed to measure
elapsed immersion time. These elements established the fundamental
functional concept of the Submariner.

The first Submariner models are historically associated with
reference 6204. Early examples were produced in 1953, although the
model is generally considered to have been publicly presented at the
Basel Watch Fair in 1954. Consequently, 1953 is recognized by Rolex
as the year of the Submariner's introduction, while 1954 is
frequently associated with its initial public commercial presentation.

The reference 6204 incorporated several characteristics that
distinguished it from conventional wristwatches of the period:

- **Case construction:** A stainless steel Oyster case designed to
provide protection against water penetration under increased external
pressure.
- **Movement:** An automatic mechanical movement, eliminating the
need for manual winding during normal use.
- **Dial:** A dark dial with luminous hour markers and hands designed
to improve readability in underwater environments.
- **Rotating bezel:** A graduated rotating bezel that allowed divers
to monitor elapsed immersion time.
- **Water resistance:** An initial guaranteed waterproofness rating
of 100 metres (330 feet).

The original Submariner had a relatively simple external
architecture. Its case did not incorporate the crown guards found on
later generations, and its bezel differed from the more elaborate
configurations introduced during subsequent technical developments.
Early examples also featured distinctive pencil-shaped hour and
minute hands, rather than the Mercedes-style hands that became
associated with later Submariner models.

The introduction of the Submariner established a functional
combination of mechanical timekeeping and underwater timing
capabilities. Its rotating bezel provided an additional measurement
function independent of the movement's normal time display, allowing
divers to monitor the duration of an immersion directly from the
wristwatch.

Rolex continued developing the model shortly after its introduction.
In 1954, the guaranteed water resistance was increased to 200 metres
(660 feet), representing an early improvement in the watch's
pressure-resistance capabilities. Subsequent developments introduced
additional modifications to the case, movement, dial, and external
components.

The Submariner's introduction in 1953 therefore represents the
starting point of a continuous technical development process.
Although its original configuration was relatively simple compared
with contemporary models, it established the principal design and
functional characteristics that would remain identifiable throughout
the collection's subsequent evolution.

## 2.2 Historical Context of Diving Watches

The development of diving watches is closely connected to the history
of underwater exploration, the evolution of diving equipment, and the
increasing demand for reliable instruments capable of operating in
submerged environments.

Before the development of specialized diving wristwatches, underwater
timekeeping was considerably more limited. Divers working with
surface-supplied air systems and traditional diving helmets could use
conventional pocket watches or other timing instruments adapted to
their equipment. However, these solutions offered limited
practicality compared with a wrist-mounted instrument that could be
consulted directly during underwater operations.

The transition toward autonomous diving equipment during the
twentieth century created new requirements for underwater
instruments. In particular, the development of self-contained
breathing apparatus allowed divers to move independently underwater
without remaining physically connected to a surface air supply.

One of the earliest important developments in this transition was the
introduction of the Omega Marine in 1932. It is recognized by Omega
as the first commercially available diving watch. Its construction
incorporated a patented double-case architecture, in which an inner
watch housing was protected by an external case designed to improve
resistance to water penetration. The watch was initially tested at a
depth of 73 metres in Lake Geneva, demonstrating an early approach to
the engineering challenges associated with underwater timekeeping.

Another important development occurred in the 1930s with the
emergence of specialized military diving instruments. The Italian
company Officine Panerai, which had supplied precision instruments to
the Royal Italian Navy, developed luminous materials and robust watch
designs intended for underwater military operations. In 1935,
prototypes of the Radiomir were commissioned for testing by the
Italian Navy. These instruments incorporated large cases and highly
luminous dial configurations designed to improve readability in
low-visibility environments.

The development of autonomous underwater breathing equipment
accelerated during the Second World War. In 1943, Jacques-Yves
Cousteau and engineer Émile Gagnan developed the Aqua-Lung, an
open-circuit compressed-air breathing system incorporating a demand
regulator. This technology allowed divers to breathe underwater
without a direct connection to the surface and contributed
significantly to the development of modern scuba diving.

The increasing use of autonomous diving equipment also changed the
requirements for wristwatches. Divers needed instruments that could
provide reliable elapsed-time measurements while remaining resistant
to water pressure, mechanical shocks, and environmental conditions
associated with underwater operations.

Following the Second World War, recreational and military diving
developed rapidly. Naval forces increasingly recognized the
importance of specialized underwater equipment for reconnaissance,
underwater construction, and other operational activities. At the
same time, the growing popularity of recreational diving created
demand for more accessible and reliable diving instruments.

These developments contributed to the emergence of a new category of
mechanical wristwatches specifically designed for underwater use.
Several technical requirements became increasingly important:

- **Water resistance:** The case and its sealing components had to
prevent water from entering the movement under increased external
pressure.
- **Legibility:** Dial markings and hands needed to remain
distinguishable in environments with limited natural illumination.
- **Elapsed-time measurement:** Divers required a practical method of
monitoring the duration of an immersion.
- **Mechanical reliability:** The movement needed to operate
consistently under conditions involving pressure, temperature
variations, and physical activity.
- **Operational practicality:** The watch had to be wearable and
accessible while using diving suits, gloves, and other underwater
equipment.
- **Structural durability:** External components needed to withstand
impacts, corrosion, and repeated exposure to saltwater.

One of the most significant developments of this period was the
introduction of the Blancpain Fifty Fathoms in 1953. Its development
involved Jean-Jacques Fiechter, then CEO of Blancpain and an
experienced recreational diver, together with French naval officers
Robert Maloubier and Claude Riffaud, who were responsible for
developing equipment requirements for the French combat diving corps.

The Fifty Fathoms incorporated several characteristics that would
become fundamental to modern diving watches, including a rotating
bezel, a highly legible dial, luminous indications, and a
water-resistant case. Its development reflected the practical
requirements identified through military diving operations.

The Rolex Submariner also emerged in 1953, during this period of
rapid development in underwater equipment and specialized mechanical
watches. Its design combined Rolex's established Oyster case
architecture with an automatic movement, a rotating timing bezel,
and a dial designed for underwater readability.

The appearance of these specialized watches during the early 1950s
marked an important transition in horology. Rather than simply
adapting conventional water-resistant wristwatches for occasional
underwater exposure, manufacturers were developing integrated
mechanical instruments specifically intended to support diving
activities.

Throughout the following decades, improvements in case construction,
sealing systems, bezel mechanisms, luminous materials, and mechanical
movements progressively expanded the capabilities of diving watches.
These developments established the technical foundations of the
modern professional diving watch category.

The historical context of diving watches therefore provides an
essential framework for understanding the introduction of the Rolex
Submariner. Its development was part of a broader technological
evolution involving underwater exploration, military requirements,
autonomous breathing equipment, and advances in mechanical
watchmaking.

## 2.3 Original Design Objectives

The original Rolex Submariner was conceived as a purpose-built
wristwatch for underwater activities. Its design reflected the
growing need for reliable instruments that could accompany divers
during immersion and provide essential timekeeping information
under conditions in which ordinary wristwatches were not sufficiently
practical. Introduced in 1953, the Submariner combined an Oyster
case, an automatic mechanical movement, and a rotating graduated
bezel in a single instrument intended for underwater use.
[Rolex, Historical Timeline](https://www.rolex.com/about-rolex/history/1953-1967);
[Rolex, Submariner](https://newsroom.rolex.com/watches/oyster-collection/submariner)

The principal design objectives can be grouped into several
functional requirements.

### Water Resistance at Diving Depths

The first objective was to provide a wristwatch capable of operating
reliably underwater. The original Submariner was guaranteed
waterproof to a depth of 100 metres (330 feet), a significant
specification for a wristwatch at the time.

This capability built upon the Oyster case architecture, which used a
screw-down caseback and winding crown to protect the movement from
water intrusion. In 1953, Rolex also introduced its Twinlock winding
crown, incorporating a double-sealing system to improve protection
at the crown, one of the most critical entry points for water.

Water resistance was therefore not simply a secondary characteristic.
It was a fundamental requirement governing the construction of the
case, crown, and other external components.
[Rolex, Mastering Waterproofness](https://www.rolex.com/about-rolex/behind-the-crown/mastering-waterproofness)

### Measurement of Immersion Time

A second objective was to provide a practical method for measuring
elapsed time underwater. During a dive, knowing the duration of
immersion is important for managing the planned dive profile and
monitoring available breathing gas.

The Submariner addressed this requirement through a rotating
graduated bezel. By aligning the bezel's reference marker with the
minute hand at the beginning of a dive, the wearer could
subsequently read the elapsed time directly from the bezel.

This arrangement provided a relatively simple timing instrument
without requiring a conventional chronograph mechanism or the
operation of additional pushers. It also allowed elapsed time to be
monitored continuously while the watch remained sealed.

The original bezel should be understood as a rotating graduated
timing scale. The unidirectional mechanism found on later
Submariner generations should not automatically be attributed to
the earliest examples.
[Rolex, Historical Timeline](https://www.rolex.com/about-rolex/history/1953-1967);
[Hodinkee, Reference Points: Understanding the Rolex Submariner](https://www.hodinkee.com/articles/rolex-submariner-reference-points)

### Legibility Underwater

Another central objective was to make the displayed time readable
in underwater environments. Visibility can decrease considerably
with depth, turbidity, and limited ambient illumination.

The original Submariner therefore used a high-contrast dial, with
light-coloured indications against a dark background, together with
luminous hands and hour markers. These characteristics helped
distinguish the principal time indications when ordinary visual
conditions were no longer available.

The arrangement of the dial was also intended to facilitate rapid
reading. The reference 6204, one of the earliest Submariner
references, featured a distinctive configuration of luminous hour
markers and relatively simple hands. Later generations refined
these elements, including the introduction of more differentiated
hand designs.

Legibility was consequently a functional design requirement rather
than merely an aesthetic characteristic.
[Sotheby's, The Rolex Submariner](https://www.sothebys.com/en/articles/g-o-a-t-the-rolex-submariner);
[Rolex, The Submariner Models](https://newsroom.rolex.com/watches/oyster-collection/submariner)

### Reliability and Mechanical Operation

The Submariner was also intended to provide dependable timekeeping
during demanding activities. Its automatic mechanical movement
allowed the watch to be wound through the motion of the wearer's
wrist, reducing the need for frequent manual winding during normal
use.

The movement had to operate within a protective case capable of
resisting the external pressure encountered underwater. This
requirement connected the performance of the mechanical movement
directly to the engineering of the case and its sealing components.

The design also avoided making underwater time measurement dependent
on operating conventional chronograph pushers. Instead, the rotating
bezel provided the required elapsed-time indication through a
comparatively simple external mechanism.

### Practicality as a Professional Instrument

The Submariner belonged to the emerging category of professional
watches developed by Rolex during the early 1950s. These watches
were intended to provide specialized functions for particular
activities, rather than serving exclusively as general-purpose
timepieces.

In the case of the Submariner, the combination of water resistance,
elapsed-time measurement, legibility, and automatic timekeeping
addressed several practical requirements associated with diving.

Its design also reflected an important engineering principle: the
watch had to function as an integrated system. The case, crown,
crystal, dial, hands, bezel, and movement each contributed to the
overall performance of the instrument.

### Summary of Original Design Objectives

The principal objectives of the original Submariner can be
summarized as follows:

| Design objective | Functional requirement | Original design approach |
|---|---|---|
| Water resistance | Protect the movement during underwater immersion | Oyster case and screw-down crown |
| Elapsed-time measurement | Monitor immersion duration | Rotating graduated bezel |
| Legibility | Read the time under reduced illumination | High-contrast dial and luminous indications |
| Mechanical reliability | Maintain timekeeping during demanding activities | Automatic mechanical movement |
| Operational simplicity | Measure elapsed time without operating chronograph pushers | Direct reading of the rotating bezel |
| Integrated construction | Combine protection and functionality in a wristwatch | Coordinated case, crown, crystal, dial, bezel, and movement design |

These objectives established the functional basis of the Submariner.
Although its construction and specifications would evolve
considerably over subsequent decades, the fundamental combination of
water resistance, legibility, and elapsed-time measurement remained
central to the model's identity.

The original Submariner should therefore be understood not simply as
a water-resistant wristwatch, but as an integrated mechanical
instrument designed to address the practical requirements of
underwater timekeeping.

## 2.4 Evolution of the Collection

Since its introduction in 1953, the Rolex Submariner has undergone a
continuous process of technical and design development. Although its
fundamental identity as a professional diving watch has remained
recognizable, successive generations have introduced changes to the
case architecture, water resistance, movements, bezels, dials,
bracelets, and available materials.

The evolution of the collection can be understood through several
major periods. These periods are not always defined by a single
reference or production year, since Rolex frequently introduced
modifications gradually and sometimes maintained different
configurations within the collection simultaneously.

### Early Development: 1953–1959

The first Submariner references established the basic design
principles of the collection. The reference 6204, introduced in
1953, featured a stainless-steel Oyster case, an automatic movement,
a dark dial with luminous indications, and a rotating graduated
bezel. Its guaranteed water resistance was 100 metres (330 feet).

During the following years, Rolex introduced successive early
references, including the 6205, 6200, 6536, and 6538. These
models incorporated changes to case construction, winding crowns,
movements, dial configurations, and water resistance. In 1954, the
guaranteed water resistance was increased to 200 metres (660 feet).

An important development occurred with the introduction of the
reference 5512 in 1959. This model incorporated crown guards, which
provided additional protection for the winding crown against
accidental impacts. The case architecture also established
proportions that would influence the design of subsequent Submariner
generations.

The early collection was characterized by relatively compact cases,
acrylic crystals, aluminium bezel inserts, and variations in dial
and hand configurations. These models established the visual and
functional foundation of the Submariner.

### Consolidation of the Design: 1959–1969

During the 1960s, the Submariner developed into a more standardized
professional diving watch. References such as the 5512 and 5513
became important representatives of the collection.

The reference 5512 was equipped with chronometer-certified movements
in several versions, while the reference 5513 represented a
non-chronometer-certified configuration. Both models retained the
traditional no-date dial layout.

During this period, Rolex continued refining the case, crown guards,
bezel markings, luminous indications, and movement architecture. The
collection also became increasingly associated with professional and
military diving applications.

A major functional change occurred in 1969, when Rolex introduced
the Submariner Date. The reference 1680 incorporated a date display
at the three o'clock position, accompanied by the Cyclops
magnifying lens over the date window.

This development created two distinct configurations within the
collection:

- **Submariner:** The traditional no-date configuration.
- **Submariner Date:** A version incorporating a date complication.

The introduction of the date display expanded the functionality of
the Submariner without fundamentally changing its recognizable
design.

### Increased Water Resistance and Modernized Construction: 1970s–1980s

The 1970s and 1980s brought important improvements to the
Submariner's technical construction.

In 1979, the Submariner Date reached a guaranteed water resistance of
300 metres (1,000 feet). The no-date Submariner received the same
rating in 1989.

During this period, Rolex also introduced new case and movement
configurations. The reference 16800, introduced around the end of the
1970s, represented an important transitional generation. It
incorporated a sapphire crystal, a unidirectional rotating bezel, and
an updated movement with a quickset date mechanism.

These modifications improved the watch's practical characteristics
and distinguished the newer generation from earlier models with
acrylic crystals and bidirectional bezels.

The collection also expanded its material configurations. In addition
to stainless steel, Rolex offered versions in yellow gold and
combinations of steel and gold, commonly referred to as Rolesor.

By the end of the 1980s, the Submariner had acquired many of the
technical characteristics associated with its modern form.

### Modern Five-Digit References: 1980s–2010

The introduction of the reference 16610 in the late 1980s marked the
beginning of a long production period for the modern Submariner Date.
It combined a 40 mm case, sapphire crystal, aluminium bezel insert,
and the automatic calibre 3135.

The corresponding no-date configuration evolved through references
such as the 14060 and 14060M.

During these decades, Rolex continued refining movement performance,
case finishing, bracelet construction, and luminous materials. The
replacement of tritium-based luminous compounds by Super-LumiNova
and subsequently Rolex's Chromalight technology reflected broader
developments in luminous display materials.

The collection also received special variations. In 2003, Rolex
introduced the reference 16610LV to commemorate the Submariner's 50th
anniversary. Its green bezel insert distinguished it from the
traditional black-bezel stainless-steel models.

This generation maintained a relatively traditional case design while
incorporating increasingly modern mechanical and manufacturing
technologies.

### Introduction of Ceramic Bezels and the Maxi Case: 2008–2020

The next major transformation began with the introduction of ceramic
Cerachrom bezel inserts in precious-metal Submariner models in 2008,
followed by stainless-steel models in 2010.

Cerachrom represented a significant change in bezel construction.
Compared with conventional aluminium inserts, ceramic offered high
resistance to scratching and better colour stability under prolonged
exposure to ultraviolet radiation.

The 2010 stainless-steel generation included the reference 116610LN,
with a black bezel, and the reference 116610LV, with a green bezel
and green dial. The latter became widely known among collectors as
the "Hulk."

These models also introduced a more substantial case design, commonly
described by collectors as the Maxi Case. Its broader lugs and crown
guards produced a different visual balance compared with the
preceding five-digit references.

Other important developments included updated bracelet construction
and the Glidelock extension system, which allowed fine adjustment of
bracelet length without requiring tools.

The no-date Submariner also received a ceramic-bezel generation,
represented by the reference 114060, introduced in 2012.

This period marked the transition from the traditional
aluminium-bezel Submariner to the modern ceramic-bezel
configuration.

### The 41 mm Generation: 2020 Onward

In 2020, Rolex introduced a redesigned Submariner collection with a
nominal case diameter of 41 mm.

The new generation included the no-date reference 124060 and the
Submariner Date reference 126610. Although the nominal diameter
increased from 40 mm to 41 mm, the case proportions were revised,
including narrower lugs compared with the preceding Maxi Case design.

The new generation also introduced updated movements:

- **Calibre 3230:** Used in the no-date Submariner.
- **Calibre 3235:** Used in the Submariner Date.

Both movements provide an approximately 70-hour power reserve and
incorporate Rolex's Chronergy escapement.

The 2020 generation retained the ceramic bezel, Oyster bracelet, and
established dial layout while incorporating changes to movement
architecture and case proportions.

The collection continued to include stainless-steel, two-tone, and
precious-metal configurations, preserving the distinction between
the traditional no-date model and the date-equipped version.

### Expansion of Materials and Configurations

In parallel with its technical development, the Submariner collection
expanded beyond its original stainless-steel configuration.

The principal material categories include:

- **Stainless steel:** The traditional configuration, originally
associated with the Submariner's professional diving purpose.
- **Yellow gold:** A precious-metal configuration introduced during
the historical development of the collection.
- **White gold:** Used in selected precious-metal versions.
- **Rolesor:** A combination of Oystersteel and gold, available in
different configurations.

The collection also developed a broader range of dial and bezel
colours. Black remained the traditional configuration, while blue and
green variations became important elements of selected models.

These variations expanded the collection's design and material
options without eliminating its recognizable visual characteristics.

### Continuity and Technical Development

Despite the changes introduced over more than seven decades, several
characteristics have remained central to the Submariner:

- A rotating graduated bezel for measuring elapsed time.
- A high-contrast dial with luminous hands and hour markers.
- An Oyster case designed to provide water resistance.
- An automatic mechanical movement.
- A screw-down winding crown.
- An Oyster bracelet in the principal metal configurations.

The evolution of the Submariner therefore reflects a combination of
continuity and technical modernization. Rather than replacing its
original design concept, Rolex progressively updated the materials,
mechanical architecture, and construction methods used to implement
it.
